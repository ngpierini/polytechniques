End Group & Pendant Group Analysis

Which technique answers which question, what each one cannot do, and the arithmetic for the three that give you a number

Three different questions

"End group analysis" covers three questions that need different instruments, and picking the wrong one is the usual reason an answer disappoints.

  • What is on the end? An identity question. Mass spectrometry answers it outright; infrared and NMR answer it if you already suspect what you are looking for.
  • How many chains carry it? A fidelity question – the one that matters before a chain extension or a block. Needs a method that counts end groups against chains, not against mass.
  • How long are the chains? A counting question. If every chain carries exactly one end group, counting end groups against repeat units gives Mn directly, with no calibration and no standards. That is the great virtue of the method and the source of its limits.

Pendant groups raise the fourth question – what fraction of the repeat units carry the modification? – which is the same integration arithmetic pointed sideways instead of at the chain ends.

Mₙ from ¹H NMR end-group integration

Count repeat units against chains. If a backbone signal comes from Hb protons per repeat unit and an end-group signal from He protons per chain, the ratio of the normalised integrals is the degree of polymerization:

DP = (Ibackbone / Hb) ÷ (Iend / He)      Mn = DP · M0 + Mend

For PMMA the OCH3 singlet is 3H.

Per chain, not per group: a telechelic with the same group at both ends contributes twice.

Both ends together – for ATRP, the initiator fragment plus the halogen.

Baseline drift and noise cost you a roughly fixed slice of the total integral. 0.1–0.5% is a realistic range on a clean spectrum.

The ceiling, and why it is where it is

End-group NMR is usually said to run out somewhere around 20 kg/mol. That is not a property of the spectrometer, it is arithmetic. The end-group signal is a fixed number of protons; the backbone grows with every unit added, so the end group's share of the spectrum falls as 1/DP. A baseline error that costs a fixed slice of the total integral therefore costs a growing fraction of the end-group peak, and that error lands directly on DP.

So the familiar ceiling is real and it has a number behind it: at Mn = 20 kg/mol a 0.2% integration error is already a 20% error in Mn, and at 40 kg/mol it is 40%. Two consequences worth acting on. Choose the end-group signal with the most protons per chain, not the most convenient one – six protons instead of two moves the whole table by a factor of three. And above about 20 kg/mol, stop treating an end-group Mn as a measurement and start treating it as a check on something else.

There is a second error the table does not show, and it runs one way. If some chains have lost their end group – termination, transfer, a failed initiation – the end-group integral is smaller than the chain count deserves, so DP and Mn come out too high. An end-group Mn above the target is therefore ambiguous in a way one below it is not: it can mean long chains, or it can mean dead ones. The distribution simulator shows what a dead-chain population does to the whole distribution, and GPC sees it directly as a low-molecular-weight tail.

Degree of substitution on a pendant group

Post-polymerization modification asks the same question sideways: of all the repeat units, what fraction now carries the new group? Integrate a signal unique to the modified unit against one carried by every unit, modified or not.

Net change: the group gained minus whatever left.

Titration: equivalent weight, and the functionality trap

A titration counts reactive groups per gram of sample. That is an equivalent weight, and it becomes a molecular weight only when you multiply by how many of those groups each chain carries. Hydroxyl, acid and amine values are all reported as mg of KOH equivalent to one gram of sample, so they share one conversion; isocyanate is reported as a mass percent instead.

EW = 56106 ÷ (hydroxyl, acid or amine value)      EW = 4202 ÷ %NCO      Mn = f · EW

When the answers disagree

  • End-group Mn above GPC Mn. Usually not a contradiction at all. A conventional GPC number is a polystyrene-equivalent molecular weight and can be off by tens of percent for any other chemistry; the end-group number is absolute. Convert the GPC value first on the calibration converter, then compare.
  • End-group Mn above the target. Suspect lost end groups before celebrating long chains – the arithmetic pushes Mn up when chains stop carrying the group being counted.
  • Titration Mn below NMR Mn. Suspect the functionality. Any monofunctional impurity, or a chain that cyclised, changes groups per chain without changing the chemistry the titration sees.
  • Everything disagrees on a branched or star polymer. Each arm carries an end group, so end-group counting returns arm length rather than molecular weight, while GPC returns something smaller than the true mass because a branched chain is compact. The chain dimensions page covers why.
