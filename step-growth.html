Step-Growth & Gel Point

Why a polyester stalls at low molar mass, and when a multifunctional formulation gels

The tyranny of the last percent

Step-growth is unforgiving in a way chain-growth is not. Any two species can react with each other, so the chain length is set entirely by how far the reaction has gone: at 90% conversion the average chain is ten units long, at 99% it is a hundred, and at 99.9% a thousand. Nothing is a polymer until the very end of the reaction, which is why a polyester or polyamide has to be driven to conversions that would be considered excellent in any other kind of chemistry and are merely adequate here.

Xn = 1 ÷ (1 − p)     (equal groups, no excess)

Stoichiometry is the second trap. An excess of one monomer eventually caps every chain end with the group in surplus, and from that point nothing can grow further no matter how long the reaction runs. Carothers' extension covers it, with r the ratio of the two functional groups taken so that it never exceeds one:

Xn = (1 + r) ÷ (1 + r − 2rp)     r = NA ÷ NB ≤ 1

A monofunctional impurity or a deliberate endcapper counts twice in that ratio, because it consumes a group and terminates a chain rather than extending it: r = NA ÷ (NB + 2NB'). This is why a 1% monofunctional contaminant is far more damaging than a 1% stoichiometric error, and why acid value on a diol delivery is worth checking before the reactor is charged.

Gel point

Add a monomer with three or more reactive groups and the chains stop being chains. Branches join branches, and at a well-defined conversion one molecule spans the whole vessel: the mixture stops flowing and cannot be recovered. Predicting that conversion is the difference between a cured part and a solid reactor.

Two models, and they disagree in a way worth understanding. Carothers asks when the number-average chain length becomes infinite, which is necessarily after the first infinite molecule appears — so it predicts gelation late. Flory–Stockmayer asks when the first infinite network appears, but assumes every reaction is intermolecular; real systems waste some conversion on intramolecular loops, which do not build the network — so it predicts gelation early. The true gel point sits between them, usually nearer Flory–Stockmayer.

What these leave out

Both models assume every group of a kind is equally reactive and stays that way. That is Flory's equal-reactivity principle, and it holds better than it has any right to for small monomers in a well-stirred melt. It fails when the second group on a monomer is deactivated by the first reacting (substitution effects), when viscosity climbs far enough that diffusion rather than chemistry sets the rate — which always happens near the gel point — and when the chains are long enough that an end group has trouble finding a partner.

Neither model knows anything about side reactions. A polyester that transesterifies, or a urethane forming allophanate above 120 °C, is building network by a route the functionality count never saw.
