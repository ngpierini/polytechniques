Chain Dimensions

How big is one chain? Persistence length, contour length and radius of gyration, from measured values quoted with their solvents

Persistence length is not a material constant

The persistence length lp is the distance over which a chain keeps pointing the same way. Below it the chain is effectively a rigid rod; far above it, direction is forgotten and the chain is a random walk. It is the single number that decides whether a polymer behaves like a rod, a coil, or something awkward in between.

It belongs to a chain in a particular solvent at a particular temperature, and for a charged chain it moves with ionic strength as well. Single-stranded DNA is about 1 nm in high salt and roughly twice that when the salt is removed and the phosphates stop being screened. Xanthan is 120 nm as an ordered helix and far more flexible once heated through its order–disorder transition. Every value in this database is therefore quoted with the conditions it was measured under, and several materials carry more than one. Where they disagree, the disagreement is the data.

⟨R²⟩ = 2 lpL − 2 lp² (1 − e−L/lp)     b = 2 lp     L = (M / M0) × lm

This is the worm-like chain, used in its exact form rather than the Gaussian approximation, so it stays correct through the rod-to-coil crossover instead of only in the coil limit. It collapses to ⟨R²⟩ = L² for a short chain and to 2 lpL for a long one, and the radius of gyration uses the matching Benoit–Doty expression.

DNA is the model system, and it is not a coincidence

Almost everything on this page is easiest to see in DNA, for three reasons that no synthetic polymer offers at once. Its persistence length is large enough to see: at about 50 nm it is a hundred times a synthetic coil’s, so a single molecule can be imaged and traced directly rather than inferred from a scattering average. Its stiffness is tunable without changing the chemistry: strip the second strand and it falls to about 1 nm, or remove the salt and electrostatic repulsion stiffens it again. And it comes in exact lengths, so contour length is known rather than averaged over a distribution.

Predicting lp from the characteristic ratio, and where that fails

For a simple aliphatic backbone the persistence length follows from the characteristic ratio C∞ by geometry alone: b = C∞ l / cos(θ/2) with θ = 180° − the backbone bond angle, and lp = b/2. Since C∞ is tabulated far more widely than lp is, that route is often the only one available.

PolymerC∞Predicted lpMeasured lpRatio

It fails for aromatic backbones, and not by a little. Bisphenol-A polycarbonate has C∞ = 2.4, which the formula turns into 0.22 nm — against a measured value near 1.0 nm. The ratio is defined per backbone bond, and a polycarbonate repeat is a rigid phenylene plus a long virtual bond, not the tetrahedral C–C the geometry assumes. Entries where the route does not apply are marked and no prediction is offered for them, rather than quietly printing a number that is wrong by a factor of four.

Measuring it: what an AFM actually sees

For DNA the most direct route to a persistence length is to look at a single molecule and trace it. The method that made that routine is set out in Vesenka and co-workers’ substrate-preparation paper: mica soaked overnight in 33 mM magnesium acetate, sonicated, then glow-discharged. The magnesium replaces potassium in the mica surface and gives the DNA phosphates something to bind to. Without that treatment the molecules are simply swept aside by the scanning stylus.

Humidity decides whether the experiment works at all. Below about 40% relative humidity a plasmid can be scanned for an hour without visible damage; above 60% only fleeting images are obtained before the molecules are pushed off the surface. That sensitivity is not a footnote about technique — it is the reason apparent contour lengths in that work agree with the sequence to 2% in one case and are 13–17% short in others.

The dimensions an AFM reports are not all equally trustworthy, and it is worth knowing which is which. Contour length is reliable: it is measured along the molecule and converted at 0.34 nm per base pair, the same figure this page uses to turn a molar mass into a contour length. Cross-section is not. The same measurements give apparent DNA widths of 10–13 nm against a true duplex diameter near 2 nm, because the image is a convolution of the molecule with a tip of roughly 10 nm radius, and apparent heights of 0.65–1.4 nm, below the true 2 nm, because a few nanonewtons of tip force flattens it. A persistence length traced from contours is sound; a diameter read off the same image is mostly a picture of the tip.

How far wrong can this go? About 60%. An early AFM study measured an apparent persistence length of 80 nm for φX-174 duplex DNA under its imaging conditions, against the 50 nm the same molecule has in solution. The same work found single-stranded poly(A) splitting into two populations, short bent molecules at an apparent 40 nm and long straight ones at 600 nm — a fifteen-fold spread within one sample, which is a good sign that what is being measured is the deposition and not the polymer.

There is a second trap in how the molecule reaches the surface. One free to rearrange equilibrates in the plane and should be fitted with two-dimensional worm-like chain statistics; one that sticks where it lands is kinetically trapped and is a flattened projection of a three-dimensional conformation, needing a different fit. Reading one with the other’s model shifts the answer by close to a factor of two. Properly equilibrated DNA on mica returns about 53 nm, consistent with solution measurements.

Done properly the method is now reliable: imaging DNA in liquid under buffer conditions that let it equilibrate recovers the correct 50 nm from a 2D worm-like chain fit, and does so across a wide range of monovalent salt concentrations rather than at one lucky condition.

Grafting side chains onto DNA stiffens it, but not in the way that helps. A DNA bottlebrush — duplex DNA carrying a dense corona of polymer side chains — has been measured by nanopore and AFM at an effective persistence length near 250 nm, around 150 nm once the DNA’s own contribution is separated out, so the coating genuinely does rigidify the main chain. But the corona thickens the molecule faster than it stiffens it, so the effective aspect ratio, lp / thickness, actually falls. That ratio, not the persistence length alone, is what governs whether a semiflexible polymer forms a lyotropic liquid crystal — a reminder that a stiffness number in isolation rarely settles a question.

One correction worth carrying: G-wires, the nanowires self-assembled from stacked guanine quadruplexes that Vesenka’s group helped introduce, are often described as stiffer than duplex DNA because each quadruplex unit is rigid. Characterisation of long multimers finds a local persistence length comparable to a single monomer. A stiff repeat unit does not make a stiff chain if the junctions between units rotate freely, which is why no G-wire entry appears in the database above: the reported values do not converge on a number worth quoting.

Where the single-chain picture ends: topology

Everything above describes one chain, alone, free to adopt any shape. Two things break that, and DNA is the system where both have been measured.

The first is that DNA has more than one persistence length. This page uses the bending value of 50 nm, which is what sets the shapes in the figure above. A double helix also resists being twisted, and its torsional persistence length is roughly 125 nm — stiffer still. Nothing on this page models torsion, and for a closed loop that omission is not small: a circular molecule cannot shed twist by rotating its ends, because it has none.

The second is topology, the subject of Davide Michieletto’s work at Edinburgh. A linear chain in a melt escapes its neighbours by sliding along its own contour; a ring has no ends to slide from. Entangled DNA becomes entangled at around 400 base pairs, so a plasmid of any practical size is well past it. The sharpest demonstration that Rg above is a dilute answer is this: simulations and light-scattering experiments on supercoiled plasmids find that supercoiling compacts a plasmid in dilute solution, as everyone expects — and swells the same plasmid when it is entangled with its neighbours, which nobody did. Larger and yet more mobile, because the supercoiled shape threads through its neighbours less. Same molecule, same sequence, same persistence length; opposite answer depending only on how crowded it is.

Because topology is set by chemistry it can also be changed on the fly. Restriction enzymes cutting supercoiled DNA to linear inside an entangled solution drive the viscosity up as they work, while fragmenting linear DNA drives it down, so a fluid can be designed whose viscosity rises and then falls on a schedule — with no change in concentration or chemistry, only in topology. Cells do the same thing deliberately, spending ATP on topoisomerases whose entire job is to cut and reseal strands.

So: treat every number above as the dilute, ideal-chain, bending-only answer. It is the right starting point and it is what scattering reports for a dilute sample. A concentrated DNA solution, a gel, or anything inside a nucleus is a topological problem before it is a conformational one.
