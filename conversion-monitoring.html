Monitoring Conversion by Aliquot

Taking samples partway through the reaction, and why an internal standard makes the numbers trustworthy

Why monitor conversion

Conversion links the recipe you designed to the polymer you actually get: every "Predicted Mₙ at X% conversion" figure in the calculator is only as good as your knowledge of the real time conversion, p. Tracking it lets you decide when to quench (hit your target Mₙ), catch a stalled reaction early, and, by plotting ln([M]₀/[M]) vs. time, check that the polymerization is behaving as a controlled/living process (a straight line means a roughly constant concentration of propagating chains, the kinetic signature of ATRP/RAFT/ROMP working as intended).

Choosing a method

MethodGood forNotes
¹H NMR Most vinyl monomers (ATRP, RAFT, FRP) and ROMP olefins Most common. Compares a shrinking monomer peak (e.g. vinyl protons) to a fixed internal standard peak. Fast, quantitative, needs only a small aliquot.
GC Volatile monomers/solvents Tracks monomer peak area vs. an internal standard peak area; good for monomers with poor/overlapping NMR signals.
Gravimetric Quick, rough estimates Dry an aliquot and weigh the solid residue vs. total mass. Simple but loses accuracy to volatile monomer/solvent evaporating unevenly. An internal standard doesn't fully fix this, so treat results as approximate.

Why an internal standard matters

Every time you pull an aliquot, you're taking an uncontrolled microvolume that's hard to reproduce. Needle dead volume, viscosity (which climbs as the reaction proceeds), and solvent evaporation during transfer all mean the aliquot's dilution isn't precisely known. If you just integrate the monomer peak on its own and compare it to a separate, earlier spectrum, differences in how much sample you actually dissolved, how much deuterated solvent you added, or the instrument's receiver gain will masquerade as changes in monomer concentration, and the data becomes unreliable.

Add a small amount of a chemically inert reference compound directly to the bulk reaction mixture before polymerization starts, at a known fixed concentration relative to the monomer. Because it's mixed homogeneously into the whole pot, every aliquot you ever withdraw, no matter its exact volume, contains the standard and the monomer in the same fixed molar ratio as the bulk. The standard doesn't react, so its peak area only tracks how much of the aliquot you happen to be looking at; the monomer peak area tracks both that and how much monomer has been consumed. Dividing one by the other cancels out the aliquot size/dilution/gain uncertainty entirely, leaving a number that only depends on real conversion.

p = 1 − (IM,t / Istd,t) ÷ (IM,0 / Istd,0)

where IM is the integral (or peak area) of a monomer signal, Istd is the integral of the internal standard signal, and the subscripts 0 / t denote the initial time point and the sampling time. Because Istd is constant per mole of standard, this ratio is invariant to how much of the aliquot ended up in the NMR tube.

Important: the standard must go into the reaction flask itself, not be spiked into each NMR/GC sample afterward. Spiking each sample separately just reintroduces the same volumetric uncertainty you were trying to eliminate.

Step by step: taking and analyzing an aliquot

  1. Add the internal standard up front. Weigh it in with the monomer/solvent charge (degassed along with everything else), at a molar ratio to monomer that gives a convenient peak size, commonly 2 to 10 mol% relative to monomer. Record the exact ratio; you'll need it (or just the t = 0 spectrum) as your reference point.
  2. Purge the sampling needle. Flush a clean needle/syringe with inert gas before it goes anywhere near the septum, so you're not injecting a plug of air into the flask.
  3. Withdraw a small aliquot (≈20 to 50 µL) through the septum under positive inert gas flow. Keep it small: enough for the analysis, not so much that you meaningfully change the monomer/agent ratio in the pot over the course of the run.
  4. Quench/dilute immediately. Expel the aliquot straight into an NMR tube containing deuterated solvent (e.g. CDCl₃) or a GC vial with solvent. For ATRP, air exposure at this point also helps terminate further reaction in the aliquot, so it's a true snapshot of that time point rather than continuing to convert while it waits for analysis.
  5. Reseal the flask and restore positive inert gas flow right away.
  6. Run the spectrum promptly. If there will be a delay before analysis, keep the sample cold (and, for radical polymerizations, consider a trace radical inhibitor) so it doesn't keep reacting and give you a falsely high reading.
  7. Integrate and calculate. Integrate the monomer signal and the internal standard signal, and apply the formula above (or track the raw ratio over time and normalize at the end). Repeat at set time intervals to build a conversion vs. time curve.
  8. Feed it back into the calculator. Drop the measured conversion into the "Conversion for predicted Mₙ" field on any technique tab to see the real time predicted Mₙ for your actual reaction progress.

Choosing an internal standard

A good standard is chemically inert to your monomer/catalyst/radicals, gives a sharp well resolved signal (a singlet is ideal), doesn't overlap the monomer, polymer backbone, solvent, or initiator/CTA peaks, and isn't so volatile that it evaporates preferentially versus the monomer during handling.

StandardTypical signal (¹H NMR)Works well with
Mesitylene~6.8 ppm (ArH, s, 3H)Methacrylates and acrylates, whose vinyl lines fall between 5.5 and 6.5 ppm. Not styrene: its singlet sits inside the broad polystyrene aromatic signal (6.2 to 7.3 ppm) and next to the styrene =CH line at 6.7 ppm
1,3,5-Trioxane~5.1 ppm (s, 6H)Methacrylates and acrylates (MMA vinyl lines at 6.10 and 5.57 ppm). Avoid with styrene, whose =CH2 doublet sits at 5.23 ppm
Anisole~3.8 ppm (OCH₃, s, 3H)Monomers without competing OCH₃/OCH₂ signals
1,3,5-Trimethoxybenzene~6.1 ppm (ArH, s, 3H); ~3.8 ppm (OCH₃, s, 9H)Monomers with nothing near 6.1 or 3.8 ppm. Poor for (meth)acrylates and acrylamides: the ArH line lands on their vinyl protons, and the OCH₃ line on a methyl ester
Hexamethyldisiloxane (HMDSO)~0.06 ppm (s, 18H)Almost any monomer, far upfield, rarely overlaps

For GC, pick a standard with a retention time and boiling point close to the monomer's (so it doesn't evaporate or elute disproportionately) but well separated from every other peak in the chromatogram.

Worked example

ATRP of methyl methacrylate, with 1,3,5-trioxane added at a 1:20 molar ratio to monomer (5 mol%). In each spectrum, set the trioxane singlet (5.14 ppm, 6H) to 6.00, so one of its protons counts 1.00. Each MMA vinyl line (6.10 and 5.57 ppm) is one proton per monomer, so at t = 0 it integrates to 20.00, the molar ratio itself (IM,0/Istd,0 = 20.00/6.00 = 3.33). At t = 2 h the same line integrates to 9.00 (IM,t/Istd,t = 9.00/6.00 = 1.50).

p = 1 − (1.50 / 3.33) = 1 − 0.45 = 0.55  →  55% conversion

Enter 55 into the "Conversion for predicted Mₙ" field on the ATRP tab (with the same target ratio you used to design the recipe) to see the real Mₙ at this point in the reaction.

What the worked example looks like on the instrument. Both vinyl lines of methyl methacrylate shrink as monomer is used up, while the trioxane line, mixed into the flask at the start, cannot change. The second aliquot was smaller, so every line in it is shorter, the standard included; that is exactly why the raw heights mean nothing and the ratio to the standard means everything. Poly(methyl methacrylate) has no signal in this window, so nothing grows in to be mistaken for monomer.

Typical shifts in CDCl3: MMA =CH2 6.10 and 5.57 ppm, 1,3,5-trioxane 5.14 ppm; residual CHCl3 7.26 ppm (Fulmer et al., Organometallics 29 (2010) 2176). Check them against your own t = 0 spectrum. Line shapes are idealized Lorentzians of equal width.

Kinetics plot

Enter your (time, conversion) data points from a series of aliquots below. This plots ln([M]₀/[M]) = −ln(1−p) against time. For a well behaved controlled/living polymerization with a roughly constant concentration of propagating chains, this should be a straight line through the origin. The slope is kp[active chains], so curvature tells you that concentration is changing, and the direction says which way: curving upward (accelerating) means active chains are still building up, from slow or continuing initiation, or an induction period while the ATRP/RAFT equilibrium sets up; curving downward (flattening out) means active chains are being lost, from termination (chains dying off) or simply from the monomer running low near high conversion.

"Active chains" here means only the small population of centers actually propagating at a given instant. In ATRP and RAFT most chains sit dormant (halide- or dithioester-capped) between activation events, so it is that active concentration, not the total number of chains, that has to stay constant. ROMP has no radicals at all; its active center is a metal-carbene (alkylidene), so read "active chains" as that active center whenever ROMP is in scope.

One exception worth knowing: in an ATRP run with no Cu(II) deactivator added up front, the first few points can curve (fast, then flattening) even when everything is working, because the deactivator has to build up before the reaction settles into its steady, linear regime. Over that early stretch ln([M]₀/[M]) actually climbs as t2/3 rather than linearly; adding a few mol% of the deactivator at the start, so the deactivator/activator ratio is about 0.1 or higher, makes the plot linear from the very beginning. So don't over-read curvature in the first couple of points.

RAFT: the radicals come from an ordinary initiator (AIBN, a peroxide), so the slope is set mainly by that initiator, not by the RAFT agent, which mostly just shuffles which chain carries the radical. Some RAFT systems (notably dithiobenzoates) show real rate retardation from side reactions of the intermediate radical, so a lower slope or gentle downward curvature can be retardation rather than a loss of control.

For the real test of control, pair this plot with a plot of Mₙ vs conversion from the same aliquots (by SEC/GPC). A straight Mₙ-vs-conversion line through the origin, together with a low and roughly constant Đ, is what actually defines a living/controlled polymerization: this first-order kinetic plot only tells you the active-chain concentration is constant, while the Mₙ line tells you the chains are really growing under control.
